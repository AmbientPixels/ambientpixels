Part 1The words

Six rules for words that ask

01

Name what happens next.

A link or button should say what the person gets when they click it. “Click Here”, “Learn More” and “Read More” make people guess, and people who have to guess often don't click.

BeforeClick Here (the student registration link in your top bar, October 8)

AfterApply for Free Job Training

02

Lead with what people get, and keep the mission beside it.

A headline is the first thing a visitor reads. Leading with the offer tells them in a few seconds why they're here. Your brand line isn't lost: it can sit right under the headline, or in a button.

BeforeWe believe in the power of jobs to change lives.

AfterFree Job Training Programs That Lead to Real Careers — Serving Five Counties in Northwest Washington

Brand lineSee How Our Programs Change Lives

03

Say “free” where it's free.

Your programs are free, and your page already says so in the body text. Put the word where the decision happens: in the headline and on the button.

04

Connect every action to the mission.

Shopping, donating and giving all fund the same work. Saying so on the button gives people a reason to choose you, and it costs only a few words.

BeforeShop Now

AfterShop Online — Every Purchase Supports Free Job Training

05

Fewer, clearer choices.

When everything has the same weight, nothing stands out. Give each section of a page one main action, and let people choose a direction before they see every option.

MenuFive main menu items: Education · Volunteer · Locations · Shop · About, plus the Give (money) and Donate (goods) buttons, which stay where they are. Every other page stays, one click further in.

06

Keep people on your site.

When a step sends someone to a different-looking website, some people stop there. Wherever you can, let them finish on your own pages. The newsletter signup below is the clearest example.

The five-second test

Show a page to someone who hasn't seen it for five seconds, then ask:

  1. What is this website about?
  2. What can I do here?
  3. Why should I do it?

If they hesitate on any of the three, the words on that page need work. It's free, it takes a minute, and it works on any page your team writes.

Before you publish a page update

  • Does every link and button say what happens next? No “Click Here”, “Learn More” or “Read More” on its own.
  • Does the headline lead with what the reader gets?
  • Does the page have one main action, and does it stand out from the rest?
  • If it's free, does the button say so?
  • Does the action connect back to the mission, in a few words?
  • Does every link stay on your site, or say clearly where it's going?
  • Would it pass the five-second test with someone outside your team?
  • Does the copy in the red top bar name its action, like “Donate your vehicle”?

Part 2The site around the words

Words are one part of it. The rest is how easy the page makes it to act. Below are the eight things your audit measured, with what good looks like for each, then the specific points from your homepage, then general good practice for any page.

What turns a visit into an action

These are the eight measures in your audit, weakest first, with your homepage's score out of 100. Use them as a lens for any page your team writes or changes.

11of 100

Ease of acting

Funnel friction

How easy is it for someone who already wants to apply, give or shop to actually do it?

  • Count the clicks from the homepage to a finished application, donation or purchase, and remove any you can.
  • Fewer choices on a page, not more (rule 05 above).
  • Keep forms short: only the fields you need, clear labels, and helpful messages when something's missing.
  • Watch for anything that pulls people away mid-step: links to other sites, pop-ups, or a second offer competing with the first.
29of 100

What the page puts first

Conversion hierarchy

Does the layout lead the eye to the most important action first?

  • Use size, color and space to make the main action the most visible thing in each section.
  • Follow a natural order: the need, how you help, proof that it works, then the ask.
  • Reveal detail as people need it. Don't put everything up front, and don't bury what matters.
41of 100

Clear buttons and links

CTA strength

Are the buttons people click easy to see, and do they say exactly what happens next?

  • Make the main buttons stand out: high contrast, and at least one visible without scrolling.
  • Start with an action word and say what people get: “Apply for Free Job Training”, not “Submit” or “Click Here”.
  • Match the button to what's around it: the words on the button should finish the promise the section makes.
  • Place buttons where people decide: after a story, after the details, at the end of a section.
59of 100

Speaks to each audience

Audience alignment

Does the language fit the person reading it?

  • Write for the person you want to act: a job seeker, a donor, a shopper or an employer each need different words.
  • Name what they're looking for (a new career, a place for their donations) before describing your programs.
  • Match the detail to the reader: enough to decide, not so much that they stop reading.
61of 100

Clear in five seconds

Messaging clarity

Can a visitor tell what you offer and why it matters within about five seconds?

  • Make the headline state a concrete benefit people can picture.
  • Prefer specifics to general claims: “free job training” over a general phrase like “making a difference”.
  • Use everyday words, and explain any program names.
  • Keep one message from headline to button, without drifting topic.
70of 100

Quick wins

Quick-win fixes

What small changes could make a real difference in a day?

  • Start with words before layout: renaming a button is a copy change, not a rebuild.
  • Your fix 01 (the Apply link and headline) is the clearest quick win on the homepage.
72of 100

Why Goodwill

Differentiation

Is it clear why someone should choose you?

  • One of your strengths. Keep saying what only you can: shopping and donating fund free job training.
  • Use words no other organization could use about itself.
79of 100

Reasons to trust

Trust signals

Does the page show you're credible?

  • Your strongest score. Keep your success stories specific: what someone achieved, with their permission.
  • Show what supports your work: partners, accreditations, years serving the community.
  • Lower the risk of acting: say what happens after someone applies, and how donations are used.
  • Make contact details, store locations and your privacy policy easy to find.

One clear path for each audience.

Job seekers, donors, shoppers and employers all arrive on the same homepage, and today it asks all four at once. Decide which action matters most there, then give each audience an easy way in, such as “I want to: Find Training · Donate · Shop · Hire Graduates”.

Fewer buttons competing for attention.

Your audit counted 14 calls to action on the homepage, including several “Read More” and “Contact” links that repeat. Keep one main action per section and give it the most visual weight.

Ask right after the story.

Your student success stories are where readers are most moved. Add an Apply or Give button directly after them, while that feeling is fresh.

Keep the main area for the main actions.

Events, like the soccer screen-printing event, are worth promoting, but in a news or events section where they don't compete with job training.

Tidy what's hidden.

A hidden second row of stats still has placeholder labels in the page code. Visitors don't see it, but search engines read it. Finish it or remove it.

Good practice for any page

  • Check the page on a phone first. Most visitors will see it there.
  • Describe every image with alt text, so screen readers and search engines can understand it.
  • Keep text easy to read against its background, including text on photos.
  • Check page speed with Google's free PageSpeed Insights.
  • Give every page a clear title and description: they're what people see in search results.
  • Make it easy to reach a person: contact details and store locations one click away.

The newsletter signup

Today the footer's “Sign up for Newsletter” link goes to a DonorPerfect form on a different website. That form collects useful things (interests, print or email, preferred store), so the right fix depends on how your team uses that information. There are three good ways to keep people on your site without losing any of it:

  1. Put the same form on your own page. Same form, same data, and the visitor never leaves evergreengoodwill.org. It needs a quick test on iPhones first.
  2. A short signup in the footer (first name, email, a few topics) through the Constant Contact plugin already installed on your site. We'd confirm with your DonorPerfect contact that signups still reach your donor records.
  3. Both: the short signup for most people, with “Manage all your preferences” linking to the full form.

Whichever you choose, keep the “I am 18 years or older” confirmation your current form uses.

Part 3Your social posts

How a post gets made

Each morning, the agents draft a few posts for the channels you already use, like Facebook, Instagram and LinkedIn. They write in your voice, using facts from your own pages. Your team reviews the drafts in one place, and nothing goes out until someone approves it.

1 · ApproveIt's ready to go as it is.
2 · Ask for a rewriteSay what to change, like “make it warmer”. A new draft comes back with the same facts.
3 · Fix a wordEdit it yourself, then approve. You're never locked into what the AI wrote.
4 · Say why notReject it with a reason. The reason becomes a note for every future draft (Part 4).

Instagram · Adult educationGoing back to school as an adult takes courage. Our free Adult Basic Education classes help adults get ready for today's workplace.

Part 4Your voice

How the agents learn to sound like Goodwill

An agent is only useful if its drafts sound like your team wrote them. That doesn't come from the AI guessing. It comes from two things your team controls, working together: a voice guide that sets the starting point, and the review loop, where your team's comments on each draft keep tuning it.

01

A voice guide your team defines.

Your team decides how the agents should sound: the tone, the words to use and avoid, the facts to lead with, and anything that's off limits. Every agent reads that guide before writing anything, and follows it.

So nobody starts from a blank page, we can draft a first version from your own website for your team to rewrite. But the guide is yours: your team writes it, owns it, and can change it at any time.

From a sample guide“Jobs change lives” is our mission line. Say “free job training”, not just “training”. Give means money; Donate means goods. Never name a student without their permission.

02

On top of that, your team's comments on every draft.

A person reviews every draft before it goes out. When someone rejects one, they say why in a sentence. That comment becomes a standing note the agents read alongside the voice guide before the next draft, so nobody has to say it twice. Over time, the notes tune the agents to how your team actually writes.

Your noteLead with free job training, every time.

Next draftFree job training, powered by your donations. Clearing out a closet? Those clothes could help someone start a new career.

What this memory is: plain-language notes your team can read, change or delete at any time. One note about your voice can apply everywhere the agents write: social posts, newsletters and replies.

What it isn't: retraining the AI. Your notes stay private to Goodwill, and a person still approves everything before it goes out.

Part 5Your newsletter

How an agent would put an issue together

The newsletter works like the social posts in Part 3: the agent drafts, and your team decides.

1 · Where it comes fromYour own website, short items your team adds during the month, and standing sections you write once.
2 · Who writes whatYour facts go in word for word. Your team can write any entry by hand, with the agent's help if they want it. The AI suggests the opening, clearly labeled for your team to rewrite.
3 · Who decidesYour team approves it, rejects it with a reason, or skips a month. Once connected, approving it sends it through your email tool, such as Constant Contact.

When you reject a draft, you say why, and the next opening follows that note. The same flow works for a regular newsletter, a campaign like Cartober that runs and then stops, and one-off announcements. It can be set up once for your external newsletter and once for an internal one for staff.

Part 6Your contact form

Buckets, owned by your teams

A general contact form brings in all kinds of messages, from all kinds of people. The simplest fix: your team agrees on a few buckets and who owns each one. The AI files every message into a bucket, and each team picks up its own.

BucketOwned byWho can see it
Store and shoppingYour team decidesThat team
DonationsYour team decidesThat team
Job training and classesYour team decidesThat team
Past employeesYour team decidesOnly the named owner: may be personal
Not sureFront deskFront desk

An example to start from. Your team names the real buckets and owners, and can change them any time.

The buckets live wherever suits your team: a shared mailbox, Teams, or a simple dashboard.

How it works

1 · FiledA message arrives. The AI reads it and files it into a bucket, with a one-line reason.
2 · Picked upEach team checks its own bucket and handles what's there. Nobody has to pass messages around.
3 · Not sure?If the AI isn't sure, it sends the message to “Not sure” instead of picking. The front desk checks only that bucket.
4 · Nothing lostA short weekly list shows anything still waiting in any bucket.

Wrong bucket? Anyone can move a message in one click. The move becomes a note the AI reads, so similar messages land in the right place next time: the same review loop as your social drafts.

The main gain isn't time. It's that every message has an owner, nothing falls through the cracks, and personal messages only reach the person who should see them.

Help with replies, if you want it

Once a message is in the right bucket, the person who picks it up can choose how much help they want, message by message:

  1. A suggested reply. The agent drafts an answer in your voice, using facts from your own website. The person edits it and sends it.
  2. Help with their own reply. They write it; the agent suggests clearer wording and points out anything they haven't answered.
  3. No help. They reply as they do today.

MessageI have a few boxes of clothes and books. Can I drop them off this weekend?

Suggested replyThank you for thinking of us! You can drop them at any of our donation centers. Hours, locations and what we accept are all on our website: [link]. Every donation helps fund free job training.

Agent notesPointed to your website for hours and accepted items, rather than guessing.

An example. The agent is told to use only what your website says, and a person checks every reply before it goes.

Nothing sends on its own: a person always reads, edits and sends the reply. Reply help can be turned off for sensitive buckets, like past employees, so those stay person to person. And when someone fixes a suggested reply, they can say why, so the next one follows it.

It would all start in the sandbox with sample messages written to look like yours, not real ones, so your team can check how it files them and what it suggests before anything real goes through it. The same helper can also sort your general info inbox, the way we showed on the call.

Next stepsGoing live, and where to start

From sandbox to live, in stages

On the call, each approved draft was posted by a person. Once the agents are connected to your accounts, that step goes away: what your team approves goes out directly, with no copying and pasting. It happens in three stages, and you decide when to move to the next one.

1 · Try it safelyThe sandbox. No accounts are connected, so nothing can post or send. Your team reviews the drafts and tells us what works.
2 · Connect your accountsWhen you're happy, someone on your team logs in to your social media and email newsletter accounts once and gives the agents permission, the same way you'd let any app use an account.
3 · Approve, and it's doneFrom then on, when your team presses Approve, the post goes up or the newsletter goes out, straight from the agents' dashboard.

Nothing publishes until a person approves it, and you can disconnect an account at any time. Each platform has its own approval process for connected apps; we'd work through it with you, and some platforms take a while to approve.

Your data. The sandbox uses only your public pages and sample messages. Before any real message goes through, we'll set out in writing where your data lives, which AI service reads it (one that doesn't use your data to train its models) and who can see it, for your IT team to approve. Replies to past employees are never AI-assisted, and those messages are seen only by their owner.

How you'll know the agents are working

In the sandbox, your team marks each draft and each filed message: yes, or “no, because”. Those marks show how often the agents get it right before anything real goes through them.

Once it's running, a few simple counts tell you how it's going: how many drafts are approved with only small edits, how many messages land in “Not sure” or get moved, and whether anything waits more than a week without an owner.
